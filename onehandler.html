OneHandler

College students often struggle to carry a to-go meal and drink at the same time, especially during busy meal times when they need a free hand to open doors, use their phones, or carry other belongings. OneHandler is a one-handed food carrier that connects a to-go box and drink into a single, easy-to-carry solution.

Students can't carry everything at once.

Every day, college students juggle to-go boxes and drinks from the dining hall, often spilling items while trying to keep their hands free. It's also a safety issue!


Pain Point 1 Students use both hands to carry a box + drink, leaving no hands for doors, phones, or navigation.
Pain Point 2 Drinks spill when opening heavy doors or walking through crowded spaces.
Pain Point 3 Current workarounds (stacking, balancing on arms) feel rushed and unsafe during peak lunch times.
Opportunity A simple connector that keeps food and drink together and secure in one hand.

Secondary Research

Pros

One handed.

Require setup time.

Market Context

The grab-and-go container market is growing fast.

Global market projected to grow from $13.9B (2024) to $18.8B by 2034.
Students represent a high-volume, design-conscious segment of this market.
Sustainability matters: students prefer recyclable/compostable materials over plastic.

Research Objective

Understand how college students currently carry food and drinks around campus, the challenges they experience, and what factors influence how they choose to carry their meals.

Field Observation

Spent time in the dining hall watching how students actually carry food.

Students stack containers and use their body or arms for extra support when carrying food.
Students hold two objects on top of each other using “Lego hands”.
Students use different fingers to hold multiple small items (drink, utensil, snack bar) at once.
Students face challenges with navigation in narrow, crowded pathways when carrying multiple items.

User Interviews

Participants: 18–22-year-old NYU students studying Biology, Psychology, Computer Science, and other majors.

Recruitment: I recruited friends and classmates at NYU who regularly eat at dining halls while moving between classes.

Why them? I chose students because they frequently move through crowded campus spaces, open doors while carrying belongings, and have to decide whether bringing a drink or snack is worth the inconvenience.


Findings

Students often skip grabbing drinks or snacks because "it's hard to carry everything."
Spills happen most when opening doors or walking through crowded spaces.
Students prefer reusable solutions but only if they don't add friction to the experience.

Exploring Solutions

I sketched dozens of concepts: rings, straps, clips, nested containers, pivoting handles. The goal was one-handed, secure, simple — and cheap enough that dining halls could mass-produce it.

Early Sketches

First round explored a wide range of attachment mechanisms: rings that hold the cup to the box, straps made from the container itself, magnetic clips, even nested designs that let you collapse the whole thing.

Narrowing Down

The purpose of this section is to show how I explored different physical ways to combine a cup and food container before deciding on a final direction.

I used this rough prototype to physically test how the combined container would feel in my hands — including its size, grip, balance, and overall comfort by taping together a regular food container and a cup.

This hands-on testing helped me narrow down which concepts felt practical and which ones had problems that still needed to be addressed.

Pros

Easy to prototype and test quickly.
Helped me understand how the combined container would feel in my hand.
Allowed me to compare different shapes and handle configurations.

Cons

Taped prototype was rough and not representative of the final material.
Some combinations felt bulky or uncomfortable to hold.
The connection between the cup and food container still needed refinement.

From Sketch to Physical Reality

I built a prototype using paper and cardboard to test the concept in real conditions. The design features a recessed ring at the top that the cup sits into, and a handle that extends from a second layer beneath the main box.

🎁

Paper + Cardboard

I chose paper for sustainability. It's biodegradable, recyclable, and has a lower carbon footprint than plastic. It also feels more premium to students — less like "disposable packaging" and more like a designed solution.

Real-World Testing

Our next phase is to place the prototype in students' hands during actual meal times. I'll observe how they use it, what breaks first, and whether it solves the problem we identified.

Reflection

Simplicity is the hardest design constraint

The best solution wasn't the cleverest one — it was the one that required the least effort from users. Every feature we added had to justify its existence by reducing friction, not adding it.

Observation beats assumptions

Watching students in the actual dining hall revealed problems we never would have discovered in interviews alone. The real pain points were physical and emotional, not just functional.

What I'd Improve

If I could restart, I'd test material durability earlier. Paper is elegant but may not survive multiple uses without getting soggy. The next iteration should explore laminated or coated paper that maintains structural integrity while staying sustainable.
